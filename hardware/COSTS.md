# Running costs: Halo scout

Generated by `budget.py` from `bom.csv` and the assumptions at the top of that script. Prices checked 2026-10-14. Every number below is either computed from `BOM.md` or carries a link. Assumptions are labelled as such.

## Assumptions

| Assumption | Value | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Drone life | 300 flight hours | assumption; 100 h and 1,000 h shown below. Covers airframe, electronics and sensors. |
| Ground station life | 300 swarm flight hours | assumption |
| Battery cycle life | 150 cycles (used below); 300 shown as a sensitivity | [300 to 500 cycles is the usual LiPo range](https://batteryskills.com/how-long-do-lipo-batteries-last/); halved because each flight uses 85% of the pack at high current |
| Flight per charge | 22.4 min | hover endurance with the integration margin, from BOM.md |
| Propellers | one set of 4 per 10 flight hours | assumption; sooner after any contact with the ground |
| Motors | one set of 4 per 150 flight hours | assumption (bearing wear in dust) |
| Charging | 90% charger efficiency, USD 0.30/kWh | assumption, set high on purpose |
| Crew | 2 people at USD 28.50/h | [O*NET 33-2011.00 Firefighters, median wage 2025 (BLS data)](https://www.onetonline.org/link/summary/33-2011.00); wages only, no overheads |

The largest line is drone hardware, amortised, USD 3.46 per drone flight hour. Battery wear comes next at USD 0.93: a USD 51.99 pack lasting 150 flights of 22 minutes. Gentler use (landing with 30% left, storing packs at storage voltage) is the cheapest saving. Crew time dominates a single drone's hour; the swarm spreads it over 16.

## Cost per hectare

Coverage rates are the simulator's measured rates on the 6 ha wildfire block, means over 20 seeds (`README.md`, wildfire table): one drone 0.5 ha/min, 16 drones 6.2 ha/min. They count scanning time only. Launch, transit to the block, battery swaps and landing add airborne time without coverage, so the table also shows a case where only half of the airborne time is scanning.

| Drones | Scan rate (ha/min) | Scanning share of flight | Hectares per hour | USD per ha, hardware | USD per ha, with crew |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 0.5 | 100% | 30 | 0.19 | 2.09 |
| 1 | 0.5 | 50% | 15 | 0.39 | 4.18 |
| 16 | 6.2 | 100% | 372 | 0.25 | 0.40 |
| 16 | 6.2 | 50% | 186 | 0.50 | 0.80 |

Per hectare of hardware, one drone is cheaper (0.19 against 0.25 USD): 16 drones cover 23 ha per drone-hour against 30 for one drone, close to the 30% extra energy the simulator reports. What the swarm buys is time. It covers 90% of the block in 44 s instead of 620 s and finds the last hotspot 13 times sooner. With crew counted the swarm is cheaper per hectare (0.40 against 2.09 USD), because the same two people cover 12 times the ground per hour.

A swarm hour with crew, USD 149, is about 4% of the cost of a contract light helicopter hour (USD 3,376) and 6% of a contract fixed-wing hour (USD 2,500). It is close to the Montana DNRC's operating cost for a Cessna patrol hour, USD 175, which is not an all-in figure.

Read these with care:

- **Not like for like.** A crewed patrol aircraft covers far more ground per hour, flies in wind and terrain a 1 kg drone cannot, and carries people and water. The swarm's job is narrower: a close thermal sweep (about 0.14 m per pixel at 20 m) of a small block after lightning, to find smouldering spots a high pass can miss. It is a complement for ground crews, not a replacement for aircraft.
- **USFS figures are 2017 dollars**, averaged over every contract of that type, with availability (standby) included. Flight-only figures are shown alongside. No inflation adjustment is applied.
- **Montana DNRC figures are the agency's own operating cost per hour** as reported to the legislature in June 2022; the report does not say what they include.
- **The swarm figures leave out** training, insurance, regulatory approval, a vehicle, software development, data links beyond the BOM, and crashes beyond what the 300-hour life absorbs. The 100-hour row above is a rough stand-in for a hard first season.
