## Inspiration

Most wildfires are caught. US fire services stop or manage 98% of them during initial attack, and the 1 to 2% that escape account for 30% or more of fire spending ([USDA Forest Service testimony, 3 August 2017](https://www.fs.usda.gov/sites/default/files/fs_media/fs_document/usda-testimony-senr-fire-christiansen-08-03-17.pdf)). Speed matters: in 9,886 CAL FIRE incidents, the chance of containing a fire within two hours fell as the response time grew ([Cardil et al. 2025, International Journal of Wildland Fire](https://www.nwfirescience.org/sites/default/files/publications/wf24160.pdf)).

Lightning fires are hard to catch early, because they can smoulder for hours before anyone sees them. Across 42 datasets, the median time from a lightning strike to the fire being detected ranges from 1.4 to 53.6 hours ([Moris et al. 2023, Earth System Science Data 15: 1151](https://doi.org/10.5194/essd-15-1151-2023)). In May 2019 a lightning strike in northern Alberta most likely started a fire at 18:08. It "held over and grew during the daytime heating", and a lookout found it at 13:22 the next day, already 20 hectares. It became the Chuckegg Creek wildfire: 350,135 hectares burnt, 98 days to bring it under control, and the Town of High Level evacuated ([Spring 2019 Wildfire Review, for the Government of Alberta, 2020](https://open.alberta.ca/dataset/5fa709d9-d70a-4176-a1b8-e097abafa9fe/resource/f42b9020-69e0-4d2c-984b-68ad33761f10/download/af-spring-2019-wildfire-review-final-report.pdf)). Agencies already hunt for these holdover fires: Alberta's patrol aircraft are "informed by lightning monitoring systems to target patrols in areas of lightning activity", and from 2015 to 2019 they made 12.2% of the province's wildfire detections, at an average of CAD 9.6 million a year (the same review, table 12), an estimated USD 51,000 for each fire they found.

We built Halo Systems for that morning-after patrol: a fire agency's detection team sends a drone swarm over the ground last night's lightning struck. In simulation, it finds most fires while they still smoulder on a few square metres, tells the crew which way each one will run, and warns the people in its path. One drone with a thermal camera takes most of a battery to sweep a 6-hectare block; sixteen do it in under a minute, if they fly together safely, keep formation in gusts and close the gap when one fails. We also wanted to know whether a language model can make the swarm's slow decisions better than hand-written rules, and how to let it try without ever putting it between the drones and their motors.

## What it does

* Does the morning-after-lightning patrol for a fire agency's detection team, in a simulated mountain valley (the wildfire valley). Sixteen drones sweep a 500 x 300 m block of the valley floor. Each flight has five fires: one already spreading in open scrub, one under tree crowns, one under a rock overhang that no downward camera can see, one smouldering in the open, and one across the valley outside the survey block. The swarm sweeps the valley floor with downward thermal cameras and watches ahead for smoke with forward cameras. As an option, the drones fly as squads over the floor and both slopes, patrolling for 12 minutes.
* Turns what the drones see into a picture for the fire crews. A ground station locates the fires from thermal finds and crossed smoke bearings, forecasts their spread with an ensemble of 16 runs of Rothermel's fire model, 3 hours ahead, and briefs the crews: how each fire was found, whether it is spreading, how fast its head runs, and which named places it reaches and when.
* Tells the crews which way each fire's head will run: a compass bearing and a cone of likely directions, worked out from the wind the swarm measured, the slope and the fuel ahead, with the reason in plain words ("pushed by the wind from SW, into dormant brush"). The crews get an update whenever the call changes, and an earlier brief is withdrawn when two finds turn out to be one fire.
* Warns the people in the fire's path, zone by zone at Japan-style levels (3 prepare, 4 evacuate, 5 emergency), each alert after an alert check, to simulated phones only. No language model takes part in an alert, and an operator can cancel an alert or give a zone the all-clear. Public alerts go out under trigger rules an alerting authority would sign in advance, and the Level 3 (prepare) rule counts a smouldering fire too; every fire found also goes to fire dispatch as a card.
* Holds every drone low for crewed firefighting aircraft, on an operator's order that passes the safety validator (shown on one development seed, not measured).
* Compares the drones with today's ways of finding fires: geostationary and polar satellites, AI cameras and calls from the public, modelled from cited figures with the benefit of the doubt (sources in `docs/WILDFIRE.md`).
* Simulates a swarm of 1 to 64 quadcopters with forward-flight power, wind and gust fronts, noisy GPS, lossy and delayed radio, motor failures, batteries, trees and buildings.
* Flies every drone with a 50 Hz controller that has slot assignment, synchronised transitions, consensus and a control-barrier-function safety filter. No language model is in this loop.
* Every 5 s of simulated time, a supervisor reads a status report and sets the formation, spacing, speed, altitude and safety margin. The supervisor is either tuned rules or Nemotron, and its answer passes a safety validator first.
* Turns plain-language mission requests into checked mission plans, and valley jobs in plain words into patrols: Nemotron reads the words, and code builds the squads. With a key, Nemotron and a keyword guard refuse non-civilian requests; without one, the regex baseline's keyword guard catches only explicit wording, so the dashboard flies only its default and example missions.
* Searches for conditions that make the swarm or the valley's ground station fail, with Nemotron as a test engineer against random search, corner search and, in the formation scenarios, the cross-entropy method.
* Benchmarks the controllers and supervisors on held-out seeds, with confidence intervals and paired comparisons, and shows it live in a 3D dashboard.
* Scouts for wildfire ignitions (the wildfire scout): a thermal sweep of a block of dry scrub for smouldering hotspots, flown with 1, 4 and 16 drones on the same seeds, so the time a swarm saves in finding every hotspot is a measured number, not a claim. The same kind of sweep searches a meadow for a missing hiker.

What is simulated: every flight, and no fire agency has reviewed or tried Halo yet. The valley simulates the fire's spread (Rothermel's surface fire model on land cover, slope and wind), plume heights, smoke and heat detection as probability models of a detector (not image recognition), the forecast, the alerts and the phones. Today's ways of finding fires are modelled from published figures, not measured. Nobody fights the fires during the flights; a first crew is modelled afterwards by a simple containment race, which overstates fire sizes. The fire model leaves out crown fire and spotting. The wildfire scout's hotspots are fixed points that do not spread, and the nine supervision scenarios have no fire. No scenario simulates the fire's effect on its own wind; gust fronts are injected to stand in for it.

## How we built it

* Python and numpy, with matplotlib for the charts. The dashboard is a standard-library web server and three.js.
* Nemotron runs on Nebius Token Factory through its OpenAI-compatible API, with a client that handles timeouts, retries, rate limits, response validation, logging and a spend guard.
* Every supervisor call is recorded, so the held-out Nemotron flights can be replayed exactly without a key. Mission-compiler and stress-search calls are kept in call logs.
* The wildfire valley adds a fire model (Rothermel's surface fire model with Albini's revisions, on Anderson's fuel models, spread over the grid by minimum travel time), a bent-over smoke plume, detection models for the thermal camera, the forward camera and the particulate sensor, and models of today's detection methods, each rule taken from a published source (`docs/WILDFIRE.md`).
* The ground station that locates fires, forecasts their spread, briefs crews and sends alerts is plain code with fixed rules. No language model takes part in it, so the valley's flights cost nothing to run. Nemotron's jobs are elsewhere: it supervises the flight, compiles missions and valley jobs from plain words, and hunts for failures. The valley's flights used the rule supervisor, apart from the squads comparison, which used none.
* The scout drone the simulator flies is a priced design in `hardware/`: a 7-inch quadcopter from off-the-shelf hobby parts, with a radiometric thermal camera looking down, an RGB camera looking ahead, a particulate sensor and a 900 MHz swarm radio, flying ArduPilot with a Raspberry Pi Zero 2 W as companion computer. It carries cameras, sensors and radios only. The folder holds a parametric CadQuery model, a parts list (USD 1,095 a drone; USD 20,281 for 16 drones, a ground station and spares; prices checked on 2026-10-14, excluding shipping and tax), running costs (USD 149 a swarm flight hour with a crew of two, assuming a 300-hour drone life) and a build guide. It also lists where the parts cannot match the simulator: the scout's GNSS error, for one, is about six times the simulator's.
* Where each loop runs, timed on a 4-core development machine, not a drone or a Jetson (`results/compute/`): the controller computes all 16 drones' commands in a median 3.3 ms of its 20 ms step; the ground station's once-a-second step takes a median 2.6 ms and a spread forecast 1.6 s; Nemotron's valley compile 4.7 s, once per job. If the cloud link drops, flight, the station, the forecast and the alerts carry on, and the hand-written rules read the valley job.
* Seeds 0-9 were used for all tuning. Seeds 1000-1019 were each flown once: the conventional configurations first, then Nemotron, after its setup had been chosen on tuning seeds and committed.
* The wildfire valley has its own seeds: 2000-2011 and 3000-3019 for development, and 4000-4019 held out and flown once with the final code. An earlier sweep on 4000-4019 was stopped part-way when the station changed, and its rows were set aside unread. The models of today's methods were checked only on seeds 3000-3003. Fire direction was tested on fresh seeds 7000-7019, flown once. Two earlier attempts are not used: one on 5000-5019 was stopped after eight one-drone flights and set aside unread, and 6000-6019 was flown before two reviews found faults in the method, which then changed (`docs/WILDFIRE.md`). Later questions each got fresh held-out seeds, flown once after a written plan: 8000-8019 (squads), 9000-9019 (pre-signed alerts), 9000-9039 (first crew: the alert flights plus 20 new valleys, modelled after the flights), 9100-9119 and 9150-9169 (the valley test engineer) and 9200-9219 (missing hiker); the valley compiler was scored once on 48 held-out requests. The aircraft hold was shown on development seed 3000 only.

## Results

All from simulated flights on held-out seeds 1000-1019, each flown once, 20 flights per scenario and configuration, apart from the stress search, which flies its own search seeds, the mission compiler, scored on a held-out set of 20 requests, and the wildfire valley. The Nemotron setup was chosen on tuning seeds and committed before any Nemotron flight on these seeds. The wildfire valley has its own held-out seeds 4000-4019 (20 valleys, 100 fires): 80 flights with 1, 4 and 16 drones, and the 16-drone flights again with alerts by distance alone, all with the improved controller and the rule supervisor. Fire direction comes from a second held-out set, 7000-7019, flown the same way; each later result names its own set. Brackets are 95% intervals.

* **Wildfire valley, finding fires.** Sixteen drones on one flight found 86% of the fires [80, 91], four drones 77% [72, 82] and one drone 60%. Sixteen drones found 59% [52, 66] of all 100 fires while they still smouldered, at about 4 m²; half of all 100 fires were reported at least 20 minutes before they started to spread (missed fires counted as never found). The two fires no downward camera can reach were found only by smoke: the one under the overhang in 75% of flights with four or sixteen drones, and the one across the valley in 20% of flights with four and 85% with sixteen. The sixteen-drone swarm also logged 0.45 false fires per flight [0.25, 0.65], and found all five fires in 8 of 20 flights.
* **Against today's ways of finding fires**, modelled from published figures with the benefit of the doubt: no cloud, a camera with a clear view of the whole valley, every satellite detection credited to the right fire, the fastest published delivery. An AI camera with a clear view, or a call from the public, reports a fire about 4 minutes after it starts to spread, at 100 to 200 m²; a geostationary satellite after 55 minutes, at 1.6 ha; polar satellites after 128 minutes, at 7.3 ha. Sixteen drones reported 60 of the 100 fires before the earliest of today's four, by a median of 69 minutes. These are models of today's methods built from cited figures, not measurements of them. Cameras and callers start their clock when the fire starts to spread, so none of them finds a smouldering fire here; the drones' lead comes mostly from that phase, and from smoulder times the scenario sets. Today's methods watch for 4 hours, while the drones fly once, for up to 40 minutes. The drones add to today's methods rather than replace them: together, today's four and sixteen drones found every fire.
* **Spread forecast.** With sixteen drones, the forecast's median matched the true spread of the fires the station knew were spreading with a Sorensen overlap of 0.71 [0.63, 0.78] at 1 hour and 0.83 [0.79, 0.86] at 3 hours (1 is a perfect match). One drone scores 0.77 and 0.89, because it finishes later and forecasts with more of the fire mapped. The forecast leaves out crown fire, spotting and the fire's effect on its own wind, each of which makes real fires faster and less predictable.
* **Which way the head runs** (seeds 7000-7019). Sixteen drones' crew briefs were a median 14 degrees [9, 20] off the true head over the next hour, against 22 [16, 28] for running straight downwind of the wind the swarm measured, and closer than that rule for 62% of the fires. The gain is modest and the intervals overlap: in this valley the wind sets most heads, and the brief adds the slope and the fuel. Straight upslope alone was about 60 degrees off. The cone held the true head for 75% of briefs, and the crews got about 12 updates per flight. The other results on this second set repeat the first: 86% of fires found, 62 of 100 reported before the earliest of today's methods by a median 62 minutes, 95.0% of reached phones warned, a median 129 minutes ahead.
* **Warnings to simulated phones.** Each flight has 63 simulated phones; 5% have no signal. With sixteen drones and today's alert rule, which also alerts on smoulders, 94.7% [93.0, 96.4] of the phones the fire reached were warned before it arrived, a median 126 minutes ahead; 32 of the 34 missed were phones without signal. That share is about the same for every swarm size and for alerts by distance alone, so it is not a gain from the swarm or the forecast. What the forecast changes is who is told to leave: against alerts by distance alone, it told 24 [18, 30] fewer phones per flight to evacuate that the fire did not reach, with the same share warned in time. Every alert passed the check (0 of 144 rejected with sixteen drones), so these flights do not show it stopping a bad one.
* **Should public alerts wait until a fire spreads?** The pre-signed rules were first built that way (seeds 9000-9019, 16 drones). Against today's rule they changed little: 141 public alerts against 148, plus 92 cards to fire dispatch (11 of them for false fires). The cost: 3 phones with signal that the fire reached got no warning in time, against none. All three were hikers reached by fires that had gone to dispatch as cards and took off after the swarm had landed. On the valley test engineer's 40 valleys (seeds 9100-9119 and 9150-9169), flown on the drawn day under both rules, of 1,176 phones the fire reached, the pre-signed rules warned 26 to 50 with signal late or never, depending on the station's random draw; today's rule, flown on the draw that gave 50, warned none of them late. Over all 60 valleys, of 1,836 phones the fire reached, the pre-signed rules warned 29 to 53 with signal late or never (about 1 in 63 to 1 in 35) and today's rule none, for about 13% more public alerts (432 against 383). So the entry's pre-signed rules alert on smoulders too: on 8 development valleys (seeds 3000-3007) their public alerts were the same as today's rule's, alert for alert, and their cards the same as the spreading-only rules'. Under that name they have not been flown on held-out seeds; the alert results above were measured under today's rule.
* **The first crew and escapes** (modelled after the flights, seeds 9000-9039, 197 fires, one engine per fire). With the drones' cards to fire dispatch, the crew reached 79 fires while they still smouldered, against none with today's reports, and the drones prevented 55 of today's 150 escapes (37%, with a 30-minute median response; 23% with a fixed 60-minute response, 36 to 56% with stronger first crews). The valley is dry and windy by design, so only that share carries over, and only to fires the drones would find while they smoulder.
* **Squads per area** (seeds 8000-8019). Squads over the floor and both slopes beat today's single sweep clearly only at 8 drones (84% of fires against 76%). From 16 up the gain is within the noise, and at 4 drones one sweep did better. Squads patrol for 12 minutes, not one crossing, and placed 0.85 to 1.50 false fires per flight from 8 drones up, against 0.35 to 0.40 for one squad on the floor. They were flown with no supervisor.
* **Aircraft inbound**, shown on development seed 3000, not measured: the operator's hold passed the safety validator, and every drone was low and nearly still 6.1 s after the order.
* **Wildfire scout, swarm size.** On 20 held-out blocks of 300 × 200 m with six fixed smouldering hotspots each, one drone found the last hotspot after 536 s on average, four drones after 180 s and sixteen after 41 s. Seed by seed, sixteen drones were 13.3 times faster than one [95% interval 12.0 to 14.5], 8.3 minutes sooner on a sweep that takes one drone 11.6 minutes, for 30% more battery energy in total. Every flight found every hotspot with no crashes.
* **Missing hiker** (seeds 9200-9219, a meadow and forest edge). Four and sixteen drones found the person in all 20 searches, 11 of them under tree crown; one drone, sped up to finish on one charge, found 16. A meadow is not a mountain, so mountain search and rescue is still next.
* **What a patrol would cost**, as an estimate from the priced design and the measured valley flights (`docs/PATROL_COST.md`). A 16-drone squad with a crew of two would cost about USD 840 a patrol day and check about 29 lightning strikes, about USD 29 a strike; a 120-day season about USD 100,000. Alberta's patrol flights cost an estimated USD 51,000 for each fire they found (2015-2019 spending and detection share, over an average season of 1,107 fires). There, a fire attacked on time that still escaped cost an average USD 2.2 million (CAD 3.1 million) to suppress, against USD 14,600 for one attacked on time and held (2011-2019, moderate hazard); in the US the escaped 1 to 2% of fires take 30% or more of fire spending, about USD 0.67 to 1.35 million each in federal suppression alone. So a squad's season pays for itself on suppression alone if it keeps one fire from escaping every 21 seasons at Alberta's figure, or every 6 to 13 at the US ones. How often it would is not measured: the first-crew model above gives a share of escapes prevented in a valley that is dry and windy by design, not a count per season.
* **Safety.** Over nine scenarios, the original formation controller lost 300 drones in crashes in 180 flights. The improved controller, supervised by the rules or by Nemotron, had no crashes and no near misses in 180 flights each. (Drones that drop out with a scripted motor failure are not counted as crashes.)
* **Supervision.** Without a supervisor, the improved controller finished 5% of forest-trail flights and 55% of urban-canyon flights, because its safety filter will not squeeze a formation through a gap it does not fit. With the rules or Nemotron it finished all of them.
* **Nemotron 3 Nano against the tuned rules:** on the five headline metrics, better beyond noise in four of nine scenarios and worse in none, but the gains are small and two come with a caveat. It held formation slightly better in the forest (86% against 85% of the time) and used 1.8% less energy in the urban canyon. When the radio degraded it finished in 66 s against 70 s, because the rules slowed down and Nano kept the plan, as flying with no supervisor does. On a low-battery leg it finished in 128 s against 138 s on 5% less energy, partly because it kept a higher speed than planned in 90 of 150 updates where its instructions said the planned speed was enough. It also took longer than the rules to settle after formation changes. In five scenarios no supervisor made a difference above the report's 1% threshold.
* **Nemotron 3 Super**, run alongside as a declared comparison, did worse than the rules in two scenarios: less time in formation in the urban canyon, and a slower, costlier low-battery leg that retired more drones.
* **Valley compiler** on 48 held-out requests: Nemotron 3 Nano with reasoning gave the right patrol for 47, with reasoning off 39, and the hand-written rules 20. Nemotron refused all 6 non-civilian requests, the rules none; the one miss was the keyword guard refusing an ordinary request before any call. Median 4.7 s per request, and USD 0.0164 for the 47 calls. 48 requests is a small set, written for the test and checked by blind readers, not by fire crews.
* **Mission compiler** on 20 held-out requests: the regex baseline got 83.9% of fields right and 9 requests fully right. Nemotron 3 Nano got 97.6% and 18. Nano with reasoning on and Super both got 100% and 20. Of the 3 harmful requests, the keyword guard refused one before any model call, and all three models refused the other two.
* **Stress testing**, 200 flights per searcher: Nemotron found 46 failures in 39 distinct conditions, against 15 for corner search, 10 for the cross-entropy method and 2 for random search. It found as many kinds of failure as corner search (13 each, only 3 of them in common), mostly by pushing every condition near its limit. In the wildfire valley it was no better (seeds 9100-9119 and 9150-9169, 40 tests per searcher): the conditions it proposed made the ground station fail 11 times, against 10 for random search and 13 for corner search, well within chance. The failure it said it expected happened in 3 of its 36 tests.
* **Safety filter ablation.** Re-flying all 1,000 stress tests without the safety filter raised the failures from 89 to 289 and the drones lost from 391 to 2,019.

## Challenges

* Nemotron 3 reasons by default. In our first live call, the reasoning used the whole token limit and the answer came back empty, and our client paid for three retries before giving up. We now switch reasoning on or off per role, stop retrying when the limit cuts a reply off, and charge every billed reply to the spend ledger.
* With reasoning off, Nemotron 3 Nano kept a grid formation that was 24 m wide, buffer included, through a 13.7 m forest trail for a whole flight. It repeated its previous answer and reason 30 times. The status report now includes the corridor and energy arithmetic, worked out by code. The rule supervisor sees the same report.
* Our first safety filter measured distances between GPS estimates and ignored GPS error, so drones still collided and had near misses in forest and canyon runs on the tuning seeds. It now pads its safety distances by two standard deviations of navigation error. Three standard deviations caused tree strikes in narrow gaps.
* In the valley, a first sweep on seeds 3000 to 3019 found that more drones gave a worse 1-hour forecast (Sorensen overlap 0.57 with one drone, 0.41 with sixteen) and an early bias of 22 minutes. With more drones, more fires were first located by smoke and sized from the plume top, which over-estimated the largest fires by up to 23 times and made some smouldering fires look like spreading ones. The station now maps burning ground with the thermal cameras, sizes a fire from direct measurements first, and uses the plume top only when at least three drones measured it well. Those seeds then became development seeds, and the held-out seeds were flown once with the final code.

## Accomplishments

* A supervisor that a 3-billion-active-parameter model runs for about $0.004 a simulated flight, with 3,400 calls and no fallbacks on the held-out seeds, and that matched or beat tuned rules on the headline metrics there.
* The held-out Nemotron flights can be replayed exactly without a key: 24 of them replayed from the published call cache match the originals apart from compute time (`results/replay_check/`).
* A valley scenario that goes from a drone's first sighting to a checked alert on a simulated phone, with the spread forecast in between, all scored against a truth the station never sees, with no language model and no key.
* A comparison with today's ways of finding fires in which every rule is written from a cited source and gives today's methods the benefit of the doubt.
* A model of the first crew that turns the drones' earlier reports into escapes prevented, with its predictions written before the run.
* A priced design for the scout itself: a CAD model, a parts list checked against the simulator's airframe, running costs and a build guide, and a model of what a patrol day costs by squad size against what an agency spends today and what an escaped fire costs (`docs/PATROL_COST.md`).
* The evidence includes the failures: the first live call that returned nothing, the run where reasoning was switched off and the model ignored the corridor check, and the scenarios where Nemotron made no difference or did worse. The first valley sweep, where more drones gave a worse forecast, is described in `docs/WILDFIRE.md`; its rows are not in the repository.

## What we learned

* Put the model where its latency does not matter, and make every output pass a validator.
* Give the model the same report as a strong baseline, and measure both on the same seeds.
* A small model without reasoning needs the arithmetic done for it. With reasoning on, it does the arithmetic itself but takes far longer.
* A model that wins in one place may not in the next. Nemotron found 2.6 times as many failing conditions as classical search in the formation scenarios, and did no better than random or corner search in the wildfire valley, on development seeds and on held-out ones.
* A number that does not move with the swarm is not the swarm's result. In the valley, 94.7% of reached phones were warned in time with one drone and with sixteen, and 95.0% with alerts by distance alone; what the forecast changed was how many phones were told to leave without need.
* When comparing with today's methods, give them the benefit of the doubt, and say where your lead comes from. In the valley it comes mostly from the smouldering phase.
* In our first-crew model, seeing a fire before it spreads is what counts: a camera that sees every fire changed today's escapes only from 150 to 147. Keeping smoulders off public alerts has a price: over 60 valleys, of 1,836 phones the fire reached, 29 to 53 with signal were warned late or never; today's rule, which alerts on smoulders, warned every phone with signal in time. So our pre-signed rules alert on smoulders too.

## What's next

* Mountain search and rescue: the missing-hiker search has been measured only on a meadow and forest edge. Next is the same search over mountain ground, with 1, 4 and 16 drones on fresh seeds.
* Re-tasking in the valley: squads now patrol for 12 minutes and an operator can move drones between them, but no drone goes back to confirm a doubtful fix or watch a fire. Have the supervisor do that, and measure whether a second look lets the station stand down the alerts for smoulders that never take off.
* Fire weather in the model: couple the fire to its own wind, which no scenario simulates yet (gust fronts stand in for it), and add crown fire and spotting to the forecast.
* Calibrate the airframe, battery and thermal-camera models against flight logs and a real camera's detection range, and the smoke and heat detection models against real footage and sensor logs.
* Write the scout's onboard program and a MAVLink bridge between the simulator's controller and ArduPilot, which the repository does not have yet. Then run the supervisor against real autopilots through a software-in-the-loop bridge, and on a Jetson at the edge.
* Model how people respond to an alert. Today an alert counts as delivered, not as acted on.
* Flying it legally. This is a pointer, not legal advice. Halo has an operator hold for crewed firefighting aircraft (shown on one development seed) and logs every order. Real flights would need the fire agency's authorisation, the incident's approval inside any wildfire flight restriction, and approval for one pilot to fly many drones beyond line of sight (in the US, a waiver for a civil operator: the [proposed Part 108 rule](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2025-08-07/html/2025-14992.htm) was not final on 21 October 2026). The README's "Flying it legally" section has the sources.

Answers to the five questions the organisers ask, with the numbers behind them in `evidence/` and `results/`.

* **What we used them for.** Nemotron 3 Nano (`nvidia/NVIDIA-Nemotron-3-Nano-30B-A3B`) and Nemotron 3 Super (`nvidia/nemotron-3-super-120b-a12b`) on Nebius Token Factory, in four roles: the swarm's slow-loop supervisor (a directive every 5 s of simulated time), the mission and valley compilers (plain words to a checked plan or patrol, with refusals), and the test engineer in the stress searches. About 13,100 requests over the project up to 14 October, 22.3 million prompt tokens, 3.6 million completion tokens, an estimated $8.23, and about USD 0.08 since, USD 0.047 of it for the valley compiler and USD 0.032 for the valley test engineer.
* **What worked well.** The OpenAI-compatible endpoint worked with a standard-library Python client and JSON mode. Nano with reasoning off answered supervisor calls in 1.17 s on average and Super in 1.37 s, with no failed calls in 6,874 on the held-out runs. The price let us measure instead of guess: a supervised flight cost about $0.004, so we could fly 20 held-out seeds per scenario and configuration and still afford tuning rounds and a 1,000-test stress search.
* **What needs work.** Reasoning is on by default, the reasoning comes back in `message.reasoning` and counts against `max_tokens`; when it runs out, `content` is null with `finish_reason: "length"`, which looks like a broken reply. Our first live check failed that way four times in 43 s before we found `enable_thinking`. A note in the quick-start, or a separate reasoning budget, would save newcomers the paid empty replies. Latency has a long tail: in one tuning round 12 of 826 Nano calls timed out at 8 s, and before we added a whole-call deadline 39 of 540 Super calls took over 30 s, the slowest 64 s. A per-request latency hint or a documented p99 would help anyone with a deadline.
* **How onboarding felt, zero to hello world.** Short, with one trap. Key in the environment, the model list returned 25 models (4 Nemotron), and the first supervisor call failed on the reasoning-token default above, four attempts in 43 s (12 October, 22:34 UTC, `evidence/first-attempt/`). With reasoning switched off by a two-line change (`evidence/thinking-off.patch`) the same check passed two minutes later, the supervisor answering in 0.97 s, and a full supervised flight made 30 calls with no fallbacks.
* **Would we build with them again, and why.** Yes. For a decision that can wait a few seconds, a 30B open model at this price and latency is the right tool, and running it on Token Factory meant no GPU to manage and every call logged with a request id for replay. We would keep the same rule: measure it against a conventional baseline on held-out seeds, because on five of nine scenarios the model made no difference, and on the compilers it clearly did (97.6% of fields right with reasoning off against 83.9% for the regex baseline on missions; 99.2% with reasoning on and 95.0% with it off, against 85.3% for hand-written rules on valley jobs).
