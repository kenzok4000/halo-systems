## Inspiration

Most wildfires are caught. US fire services stop or manage 98% of them during initial attack, and the 1 to 2% that escape account for 30% or more of fire spending ([USDA Forest Service testimony, 3 August 2017](https://www.fs.usda.gov/sites/default/files/fs_media/fs_document/usda-testimony-senr-fire-christiansen-08-03-17.pdf)). Speed matters: in 9,886 CAL FIRE incidents, the chance of containing a fire within two hours fell as the response time grew ([Cardil et al. 2025, International Journal of Wildland Fire](https://www.nwfirescience.org/sites/default/files/publications/wf24160.pdf)).

Lightning fires are hard to catch early, because they can smoulder for hours before anyone sees them. Across 42 datasets, the median time from a lightning strike to the fire being detected ranges from 1.4 to 53.6 hours ([Moris et al. 2023, Earth System Science Data 15: 1151](https://doi.org/10.5194/essd-15-1151-2023)). In May 2019 a lightning strike in northern Alberta most likely started a fire at 18:08. It "held over and grew during the daytime heating", and a lookout found it at 13:22 the next day, already 20 hectares. It became the Chuckegg Creek wildfire: 350,135 hectares burnt, 98 days to bring it under control, and the Town of High Level evacuated ([Spring 2019 Wildfire Review, for the Government of Alberta, 2020](https://open.alberta.ca/dataset/5fa709d9-d70a-4176-a1b8-e097abafa9fe/resource/f42b9020-69e0-4d2c-984b-68ad33761f10/download/af-spring-2019-wildfire-review-final-report.pdf)). Agencies already hunt for these holdover fires: Alberta's patrol aircraft are "informed by lightning monitoring systems to target patrols in areas of lightning activity", and from 2015 to 2019 they made 12.2% of the province's wildfire detections, at an average of CAD 9.6 million a year (the same review, table 12), an estimated USD 51,000 for each fire they found.

We built Halo Systems for that morning-after patrol: a fire agency's detection team sends a drone swarm over the ground last night's lightning struck. In simulation, it finds most fires while they still smoulder on a few square metres, tells the crew which way each one will run, and warns the people in its path. One drone with a thermal camera takes most of a battery to sweep a 6-hectare block; sixteen do it in under a minute, if they fly together safely, keep formation in gusts and close the gap when one fails. We also wanted to know whether a language model can make the swarm's slow decisions better than hand-written rules, and how to let it try without ever putting it between the drones and their motors.

## What it does

* **Does the morning-after-lightning patrol for a fire agency's detection team, in a simulated mountain valley.** Sixteen drones sweep a 500 x 300 m block of the valley floor with downward thermal cameras and watch ahead for smoke with forward cameras. Each flight has five fires: one already spreading in open scrub, one under tree crowns, one under a rock overhang that no downward camera can see, one smouldering in the open, and one across the valley outside the survey block. As options, the drones fly as squads over the floor and both slopes for a 12-minute patrol, and a team of 4 flies to the reported lightning strikes of the last two nights one at a time, patrolling a 120 m square round each.
* **Turns what the drones see into a picture for the crews.** A ground station locates the fires from thermal finds and crossed smoke bearings, forecasts their spread 3 hours ahead with an ensemble of 16 runs of Rothermel's fire model, and briefs the crews: how each fire was found, whether it is spreading, how fast and which way its head runs (a compass bearing with a cone of likely directions, worked out from the slope, the fuel ahead and the measured wind, which in the simulation is the true wind plus noise, with the reason in plain words: "pushed by the wind from SW, into dormant brush") and which named places it reaches and when. The brief is also a map file (GeoJSON or KML) and a short message in the NWCG size-up order; the crews get an update whenever the call changes, and a brief is withdrawn when two finds turn out to be one fire.
* **Sends every fire found to fire dispatch as a card, and warns the people in the fire's path**, zone by zone at Japan-style levels (3 prepare, 4 evacuate, 5 emergency), under trigger rules an alerting authority would sign in advance; the Level 3 rule counts a smouldering fire too. Each alert passes a check, names its rule and goes to simulated phones only. No language model takes part in an alert; an operator can cancel an alert or give a zone the all-clear, and a one-tap approval mode exists.
* **Compares the drones with today's ways of finding fires**: geostationary and polar satellites, AI cameras and calls from the public, modelled from cited figures with the benefit of the doubt (sources in [docs/WILDFIRE.md](https://github.com/kenzok4000/halo-systems/blob/main/docs/WILDFIRE.md)).
* **Flies a supervised swarm.** A 50 Hz controller with slot assignment, synchronised transitions, consensus and a control-barrier-function safety filter flies every drone; no language model is in that loop. Every 5 s of simulated time a supervisor, tuned rules or Nemotron, reads the same status report and sets the formation, spacing, speed, altitude and safety margin, and its answer passes a safety validator first. On an operator's order, checked the same way, every drone holds low for crewed firefighting aircraft (shown on one development seed, not measured).
* **Gives Nemotron five roles, each measured against a baseline.** It turns plain-language mission requests into checked mission plans, and valley jobs in plain words into patrols: Nemotron reads the words and code builds the squads. With a key, Nemotron and a keyword guard refuse non-civilian requests; without one, the regex baseline's guard catches only explicit wording, so the dashboard flies only its default and example missions; a valley job that is not in the recording is read by those rules and can still be flown as an ordinary patrol, but a valley plan can only set the drones, the areas, the patrol length and which strikes to check. It supervises the formation in nine flight scenarios. As a test engineer it searches for conditions that make the swarm or the valley's ground station fail, against random search, corner search and, in the formation scenarios, the cross-entropy method. For the optional check team it picks the next lightning strike, with a one-sentence reason, from a table code builds of the strikes the team can reach in time; the safety validator checks every pick, code flies the team, and a hand-written triage rule, flown beside it, found as many fires.
* **Simulates the conditions a scouting swarm flies through**: 1 to 64 quadcopters with forward-flight power, wind and gust fronts, noisy GPS, lossy and delayed radio, motor failures, batteries, trees and buildings. It benchmarks the controllers and supervisors on held-out seeds, with confidence intervals and paired comparisons, and shows it all live in a 3D dashboard.
* **Also sweeps a block of dry scrub for smouldering hotspots** (the wildfire scout) with 1, 4 and 16 drones on the same seeds, so the time a swarm saves is a measured number, not a claim; the same kind of sweep searches a meadow for a missing hiker.

What is simulated: every flight, and no fire agency has reviewed or tried Halo yet. The valley simulates the fire's spread (Rothermel's surface fire model on land cover, slope and wind), plume heights, smoke and heat detection as probability models of a detector (not image recognition), the forecast, the alerts and the phones. Today's ways of finding fires are modelled from published figures, not measured. Patrol aircraft and lookouts are not in that comparison, because no source we found gives when patrols fly or how soon a lookout sees a smoulder's thin plume; patrol aircraft appear only in the cost comparison, chiefly as Alberta's cost per fire found ([docs/PATROL_COST.md](https://github.com/kenzok4000/halo-systems/blob/main/docs/PATROL_COST.md)). Thermal clutter (sun-heated rock, vehicles, campfires) is not modelled, so a thermal find is always a true fire. Nobody fights the fires during the flights; a first crew is modelled afterwards by a simple containment race, which overstates fire sizes. The fire model leaves out crown fire and spotting. The wildfire scout's hotspots are fixed points that do not spread, and the nine supervision scenarios have no fire. No scenario simulates the fire's effect on its own wind; gust fronts are injected to stand in for it.

## How we built it

* Python and numpy, with matplotlib for the charts. The dashboard is a standard-library web server and three.js.
* Nemotron runs on Nebius Token Factory through its OpenAI-compatible API, with a client that handles timeouts, retries, rate limits, response validation, logging and a spend guard.
* Every supervisor call is recorded, so the held-out Nemotron flights can be replayed exactly without a key, and so are the valley compiler's and the strike picker's calls. Mission-compiler and stress-search calls are kept in call logs.
* The wildfire valley adds a fire model (Rothermel's surface fire model with Albini's revisions, on Anderson's fuel models, spread over the grid by minimum travel time), a bent-over smoke plume, detection models for the thermal camera, the forward camera and the particulate sensor, and models of today's detection methods, each rule taken from a published source ([docs/WILDFIRE.md](https://github.com/kenzok4000/halo-systems/blob/main/docs/WILDFIRE.md)).
* The ground station that locates fires, forecasts their spread, briefs crews and sends alerts is plain code with fixed rules. No language model takes part in it, so the valley's flights cost nothing to run, apart from the strike checks' Nemotron picks (USD 0.086 for 20 flights). Nemotron's jobs are elsewhere: it supervises the flight, compiles missions and valley jobs from plain words, hunts for failures and, for an optional check team, picks the next lightning strike to look at. The valley's flights used the rule supervisor, apart from the squads comparison and the strike checks, which used none.
* The scout drone the simulator flies is a priced design in [hardware/](https://github.com/kenzok4000/halo-systems/tree/main/hardware): a 7-inch quadcopter from off-the-shelf hobby parts, with a radiometric thermal camera looking down, an RGB camera looking ahead, a particulate sensor and a 900 MHz swarm radio, flying ArduPilot with a Raspberry Pi Zero 2 W as companion computer. It carries cameras, sensors and radios only. The folder holds a parametric CadQuery model, a parts list (USD 1,095 a drone; USD 20,281 for 16 drones, a ground station and spares; prices checked on 2026-10-14, excluding shipping and tax), running costs (USD 149 a swarm flight hour with a crew of two, assuming a 300-hour drone life) and a build guide. It also lists where the parts cannot match the simulator: the scout's GNSS error, for one, is about six times the simulator's.
* Where each loop runs, timed on a 4-core development machine, not a drone or a Jetson ([results/compute/](https://github.com/kenzok4000/halo-systems/tree/main/results/compute)): the controller computes all 16 drones' commands in a median 3.3 ms of its 20 ms step; the ground station's once-a-second step takes a median 2.6 ms and a spread forecast 1.6 s; Nemotron's valley compile 4.7 s, once per job, and a strike pick 10.8 s (the slowest 156 s), while the check team keeps patrolling. If the cloud link drops, flight, the station, the forecast and the alerts carry on, the hand-written rules read the valley job, and a check team keeps patrolling its square.
* Seeds 0-9 were used for all tuning. Seeds 1000-1019 were each flown once: the conventional configurations first, then Nemotron, after its setup had been chosen on tuning seeds and committed.
* The wildfire valley was built on development seeds 2000-2011 and 3000-3019. Every held-out result comes from seeds never used in building it, each flown once: 4000-4019 for the valley, 7000-7019 for fire direction, and fresh seeds, flown after a written plan, for each later question. One comparison was added after its run: the valley test engineer's 40 valleys were flown again under the waiting rules and today's rule. Earlier attempts that were stopped or found faulty were set aside and are not used. The aircraft hold (seed 3000), the controller and ground-station timings on the development machine (3000-3003), an 8-valley alert check and the valley test engineer's trial searches (both 3000-3007) use development seeds only. Every held-out seed set and its use is in [results/README.md](https://github.com/kenzok4000/halo-systems/blob/main/results/README.md).

## Results

All from simulated flights, flown once on held-out seeds except where a bullet says development seeds or modelled; each measured result names its seed set, brackets are 95% intervals, and the full tables are in [docs/RESULTS.md](https://github.com/kenzok4000/halo-systems/blob/main/docs/RESULTS.md).

**The wildfire valley** (seeds 4000-4019: 20 valleys, 100 fires; 80 flights with 1, 4 and 16 drones, and the 16-drone flights again with alerts by distance alone, all with the improved controller and the rule supervisor; fire direction from a second held-out set, 7000-7019, flown the same way)

* **Finding fires.** Sixteen drones on one flight found 86% of the fires [80, 91], four drones 77% [72, 82] and one drone 60%. Sixteen drones found 59% [52, 66] of all 100 fires while they still smouldered, at about 4 m²; half of all 100 fires were reported at least 20 minutes before they started to spread (missed fires counted as never found). The two fires no downward camera can reach were found only by smoke: the one under the overhang in 75% of flights with four or sixteen drones, and the one across the valley in 20% of flights with four and 85% with sixteen. The sixteen-drone swarm also logged 0.45 false fires per flight [0.25, 0.65], and found all five fires in 8 of 20 flights.
* **Against today's ways of finding fires.** Sixteen drones reported 60 of the 100 fires before the earliest of today's four methods, by a median of 69 minutes (62 of 100, by 62 minutes, on seeds 7000-7019). Today's four (geostationary and polar satellites, AI cameras and calls from the public) are modelled from published figures with the benefit of the doubt: no cloud, a camera with a clear view of the whole valley, every satellite detection credited to the right fire, the fastest published delivery. An AI camera with a clear view, or a call from the public, reports a fire about 4 minutes after it starts to spread, at 100 to 200 m²; a geostationary satellite after 55 minutes, at 1.6 ha; polar satellites after 128 minutes, at 7.3 ha. Today's methods watch for 4 hours, while the drones fly once, for up to 40 minutes; the drones add to today's methods rather than replace them: together, today's four and sixteen drones found every fire.
* **Where the lead comes from.** Cameras and callers are modelled to start looking when a fire starts to spread, so none of them finds a smouldering fire here, and the drones' lead comes mostly from that phase, whose length the scenario sets. On the fires that started to spread within 20 minutes of take-off, sixteen drones were still first on 8 of 9 (seeds 4000-4019) and 9 of 12 (7000-7019), by a median of 17 and 13 minutes, in a breakdown of the same flights made after the results were known ([results/wildfire_spread_timing/](https://github.com/kenzok4000/halo-systems/tree/main/results/wildfire_spread_timing)).
* **Spread forecast, and which way the head runs.** The forecast's median matched the true spread with a Sorensen overlap of 0.71 [0.63, 0.78] at 1 hour and 0.83 [0.79, 0.86] at 3 hours (1 is a perfect match), with sixteen drones, on the fires the station knew were spreading; one drone scores 0.77 and 0.89, because it finishes later and forecasts with more of the fire mapped. On seeds 7000-7019, sixteen drones' crew briefs were a median 14 degrees [9, 20] off the true head over the next hour, against 22 [16, 28] for running straight downwind of the same measured wind (modelled as the true wind with 0.5 m/s and 5 degrees of noise), and closer than that rule for 62% of the fires. The gain is modest and the intervals overlap: in this valley the wind sets most heads, and the brief adds the slope and the fuel (straight upslope alone was about 60 degrees off). The cone held the true head for 75% of briefs, and the crews got about 12 updates per flight. The forecast leaves out crown fire, spotting and the fire's effect on its own wind, each of which makes real fires faster and less predictable.
* **Warnings to simulated phones.** 94.7% [93.0, 96.4] of the phones the fire reached were warned before it arrived, a median 126 minutes ahead, with sixteen drones under today's alert rule, which also alerts on smoulders; 32 of the 34 missed were phones without signal. Each flight has 63 simulated phones; 5% have no signal. The share warned in time is about the same for every swarm size and for alerts by distance alone, so it is not a gain from the swarm or the forecast. What the forecast changes is who is told to leave: against alerts by distance alone, it told 24 [18, 30] fewer phones per flight to evacuate that the fire did not reach, with the same share warned in time. Every alert passed the check (0 of 144 rejected with sixteen drones), so these flights do not show it stopping a bad one. On the second set (seeds 7000-7019) the other results repeat the first: 86% of fires found, 95.0% of reached phones warned, a median 129 minutes ahead.

**Crews, escapes and the alert design**

* **Should public alerts wait until a fire spreads? No.** Over 60 held-out valleys (seeds 9000-9019, 9100-9119 and 9150-9169, 16 drones), the fire reached 1,836 phones. Pre-signed rules that wait for spreading (the waiting rules) warned 29 to 53 of those with signal late or never (about 1 in 63 to 1 in 35, depending on the station's random draw); today's rule warned none late or never, at the cost of about 13% more public alerts (432 against 383). The pre-signed rules were first built as waiting rules (seeds 9000-9019). Against today's rule they changed little: 141 public alerts against 148, plus 92 cards to fire dispatch (11 of them for false fires), but 3 phones with signal that the fire reached got no warning in time, against none; all three were hikers reached by fires that had gone to dispatch as cards and took off after the swarm had landed. On the valley test engineer's 40 valleys (seeds 9100-9119 and 9150-9169), flown again after that run in each valley's own conditions (no stress applied) under both rules, of 1,176 phones the fire reached, the waiting rules warned 26 to 50 with signal late or never, depending on the draw; today's rule, flown on the draw that gave 50, warned none of them late.
* **The entry's alert design, flown once on held-out valleys.** The entry's pre-signed rules therefore alert on smoulders too. On 20 held-out valleys flown once after that choice (seeds 9400-9419, the plan written first), their public alerts were the same as today's rule's in every valley, alert for alert: 140 alerts, 561 of 588 phones the fire reached warned in time, none with signal missed, and a median lead of 116 minutes, under both. Their card counts to fire dispatch (cards, and cards for false fires) matched the waiting rules' in every valley: 101 cards, 10 of them for false fires ([results/wildfire_alerts_smoulders/](https://github.com/kenzok4000/halo-systems/tree/main/results/wildfire_alerts_smoulders)). On 8 development valleys (seeds 3000-3007) the public alerts were likewise identical, alert for alert, and the card counts matched. So the alert results above, measured under today's rule, are what the entry's design is expected to give by construction: once smoulders count, its Level 3 rule is the same rule as today's. The two were identical alert for alert in all 28 valleys flown under both (20 held-out, 8 development), as the code implies. An official's one-tap approval, measured on seeds 9000-9019 beside the waiting rules, sent 23 fewer public alerts than those rules sent automatically (118 against 141) and 30 fewer than today's rule, and one more phone with signal was reached before any warning (4 against 3); the entry's design with one-tap approval has not been measured.
* **The first crew and escapes** (modelled after the flights, seeds 9000-9039, 197 fires, one engine per fire). With the drones' cards to fire dispatch, the crew reached 40% of the fires while they still smouldered, against none with today's reports, and the drones' reports prevented 37% of today's escapes (with a 30-minute median response; 23% with a fixed 60-minute response, 36 to 56% with stronger first crews). The valley is dry and windy by design, so only that share carries over, and only to fires the drones would find while they smoulder.

**Scale and cost**

* **Squads per area** (seeds 8000-8019, flown with no supervisor). Squads over the floor and both slopes beat the entry's default flight, one sweep of the floor, clearly only at 8 drones (84% of fires against 76%); from 16 up the gain is within the noise, and at 4 drones one sweep did better. Squads patrol for 12 minutes rather than one crossing, and placed 0.85 to 1.50 false fires per flight from 8 drones up, against 0.35 to 0.40 for a single squad patrolling the floor for the same 12 minutes.
* **A team checking last night's strikes** (seeds 9300-9319, 140 flights, flown with no supervisor). With 8 drones, a team of 4 checking lightning strikes one at a time found 96% of fires, against 90% with every drone patrolling an area: +0.3 per flight, at the edge of the noise (interval from +0.00), mostly the fire across the valley, with less than half the false fires (0.60 to 0.65 per flight against 1.50); with 16 drones the team added nothing measurable (96% against 95%). Between Nemotron and a hand-written triage rule picking the strikes there was no measurable difference: 96 of 100 fires each, +0.00 per flight (-0.20 to +0.20); Nemotron's 77 calls cost USD 0.086. No flight crashed or logged a near miss, but the team brought two drones as close as 5.1 m, against 10.9 m with no team; where those close passes come from was not traced.
* **What a patrol would cost**, as an estimate from the priced design and the measured valley flights ([docs/PATROL_COST.md](https://github.com/kenzok4000/halo-systems/blob/main/docs/PATROL_COST.md)). A 16-drone squad with a crew of two would cost about USD 840 a patrol day and check about 29 lightning strikes, about USD 29 a strike; a 120-day season about USD 100,000. Alberta's patrol flights cost an estimated USD 51,000 for each fire they found (2015-2019 spending and detection share, over an average season of 1,107 fires). There, a fire attacked on time that still escaped cost an average USD 2.2 million (CAD 3.1 million) to suppress, against USD 14,600 for one attacked on time and held (2011-2019, moderate hazard); in the US the escaped 1 to 2% of fires take 30% or more of fire spending, about USD 0.67 to 1.35 million each in federal suppression alone. So a squad's season pays for itself on suppression alone if it keeps one fire from escaping every 21 seasons at Alberta's figure, or every 6 to 13 at the US ones. How often it would is not measured: the first-crew model above gives a share of escapes prevented in a valley that is dry and windy by design, not a count per season.

**The swarm and the model** (seeds 1000-1019, 20 flights per scenario and configuration, with the Nemotron setup chosen on tuning seeds and committed before any Nemotron flight on them; the stress search flies its own seeds, 0-39 (which include the tuning seeds 0-9) and four sets from 10007 to 40067, and the compilers are scored on held-out requests)

* **Safety and supervision.** Over nine scenarios, the original formation controller lost 300 drones in crashes in 180 flights. The improved controller, supervised by the rules or by Nemotron, had no crashes and no near misses in 180 flights each (drones that drop out with a scripted motor failure are not counted as crashes). Without a supervisor, the improved controller finished 5% of forest-trail flights and 55% of urban-canyon flights, because its safety filter will not squeeze a formation through a gap it does not fit; with the rules or Nemotron it finished all of them. Re-flying all 1,000 stress tests without the safety filter raised the failures from 89 to 289 and the drones lost from 391 to 2,019.
* **Nemotron 3 Nano against the tuned rules:** on the five headline metrics, better beyond noise in four of nine scenarios and worse in none, but the gains are small and two come with a caveat. It held formation slightly better in the forest (86% against 85% of the time) and used 1.8% less energy in the urban canyon. When the radio degraded it finished in 66 s against 70 s, because the rules slowed down and Nano kept the plan, as flying with no supervisor does. On a low-battery leg it finished in 128 s against 138 s on 5% less energy, partly because it kept a higher speed than planned in 90 of 150 updates where its instructions said the planned speed was enough. It also took longer than the rules to settle after formation changes, and in five scenarios no supervisor made a difference above the report's 1% threshold. Nemotron 3 Super, run alongside as a declared comparison, did worse than the rules in two scenarios: less time in formation in the urban canyon, and a slower, costlier low-battery leg that retired more drones.
* **Plain words to a checked plan.** Valley compiler, 48 held-out requests: Nemotron 3 Nano with reasoning gave the right patrol for 47, with reasoning off 39, and the hand-written rules 20, which lost mostly on patrol lengths written in words (17 of their 28 misses); 15 of the 28 differ only in patrol length (14 of them 12 against 15 minutes), and counting those as the same flight the rules score 35 of 48. Nemotron refused all 6 non-civilian requests, the rules none; the one miss was the keyword guard refusing an ordinary request before any call. Median 4.7 s per request, and USD 0.0164 for the 47 calls. 48 requests is a small set, written for the test and checked by blind readers, not by fire crews. Mission compiler, 20 held-out requests: the regex baseline got 83.9% of fields right and 9 requests fully right; Nemotron 3 Nano got 97.6% and 18; Nano with reasoning on and Super both got 100% and 20. Of the 3 harmful requests, the keyword guard refused one before any model call, and all three models refused the other two.
* **Nemotron as test engineer**, 200 flights per searcher: it found 46 failures in 39 distinct conditions, against 15 for corner search, 10 for the cross-entropy method and 2 for random search, and as many kinds of failure as corner search (13 each, only 3 of them in common), mostly by pushing every condition near its limit. In the wildfire valley it was no better (seeds 9100-9119 and 9150-9169, 40 tests per searcher): the conditions it proposed made the ground station fail 11 times, against 10 for random search and 13 for corner search, well within chance. The failure it said it expected happened in 3 of its 36 tests.

**Also measured, and one demonstration**

* **Wildfire scout, swarm size** (seeds 1000-1019). On 20 held-out blocks of 300 × 200 m with six fixed smouldering hotspots each, one drone found the last hotspot after 536 s on average, four drones after 180 s and sixteen after 41 s. Seed by seed, sixteen drones were 13.3 times faster than one [95% interval 12.0 to 14.5], 8.3 minutes sooner on a sweep that takes one drone 11.6 minutes, for 30% more battery energy in total. Every flight found every hotspot with no crashes.
* **Missing hiker** (seeds 9200-9219, a meadow and forest edge). Four and sixteen drones found the person in all 20 searches, 11 of them under tree crown; one drone, sped up to finish on one charge, found 16. A meadow is not a mountain, so mountain search and rescue is still next.
* **Aircraft inbound**, shown on development seed 3000, not measured: the operator's hold passed the safety validator, and every drone was low and nearly still 6.1 s after the order. The hold keeps the drones where they are, 10 m above the ground or canopy, rather than clearing them to an agreed block or landing them, and the 5-minute hold used about 28.7% of a battery per drone.

## Challenges

* Nemotron 3 reasons by default. In our first live call, the reasoning used the whole token limit and the answer came back empty, and our client paid for three retries before giving up. We now switch reasoning on or off per role, stop retrying when the limit cuts a reply off, and charge every billed reply to the spend ledger.
* With reasoning off, Nemotron 3 Nano kept a grid formation that was 24 m wide, buffer included, through a 13.7 m forest trail for a whole flight. It repeated its previous answer and reason 30 times. The status report now includes the corridor and energy arithmetic, worked out by code. The rule supervisor sees the same report.
* Our first safety filter measured distances between GPS estimates and ignored GPS error, so drones still collided and had near misses in forest and canyon runs on the tuning seeds. It now pads its safety distances by two standard deviations of navigation error. Three standard deviations caused tree strikes in narrow gaps.
* In the valley, a first sweep on seeds 3000 to 3019 found that more drones gave a worse 1-hour forecast (Sorensen overlap 0.57 with one drone, 0.41 with sixteen) and an early bias of 22 minutes. With more drones, more fires were first located by smoke and sized from the plume top, which over-estimated the largest fires by up to 23 times and made some smouldering fires look like spreading ones. The station now maps burning ground with the thermal cameras, sizes a fire from direct measurements first, and uses the plume top only when at least three drones measured it well. Those seeds then became development seeds, and the held-out seeds were flown once with the final code.

## Accomplishments

* A supervisor that a 3-billion-active-parameter model runs for about $0.004 a simulated flight, with 3,400 calls and no fallbacks on the held-out seeds, and that matched or beat tuned rules on the headline metrics there.
* The held-out Nemotron flights can be replayed exactly without a key: 24 of them replayed from the published call cache match the originals apart from compute time ([results/replay_check/](https://github.com/kenzok4000/halo-systems/tree/main/results/replay_check)).
* A valley scenario that goes from a drone's first sighting to a checked alert on a simulated phone, with the spread forecast in between, all scored against a truth the station never sees, with no language model and no key.
* A comparison with today's ways of finding fires in which every rule is written from a cited source and gives today's methods the benefit of the doubt.
* A model of the first crew that turns the drones' earlier reports into escapes prevented, with its predictions written before the run.
* A priced design for the scout itself: a CAD model, a parts list checked against the simulator's airframe, running costs and a build guide, and a model of what a patrol day costs by squad size against what an agency spends today and what an escaped fire costs ([docs/PATROL_COST.md](https://github.com/kenzok4000/halo-systems/blob/main/docs/PATROL_COST.md)).
* The evidence includes the failures: the first live call that returned nothing, the run where reasoning was switched off and the model ignored the corridor check, and the scenarios where Nemotron made no difference or did worse. The first valley sweep, where more drones gave a worse forecast, is described in [docs/WILDFIRE.md](https://github.com/kenzok4000/halo-systems/blob/main/docs/WILDFIRE.md); its rows are not in the repository.

## What we learned

* Put the model where its latency does not matter, and make every output pass a validator.
* Give the model the same report as a strong baseline, and measure both on the same seeds.
* A small model without reasoning needs the arithmetic done for it. With reasoning on, it does the arithmetic itself but takes far longer.
* A model that wins in one place may not in the next. Nemotron found 2.6 times as many failing conditions as classical search in the formation scenarios, and did no better than random or corner search in the wildfire valley, on development seeds and on held-out ones. Picking lightning strikes for a check team, it did no better than a hand-written triage rule.
* A number that does not move with the swarm is not the swarm's result. In the valley, 94.7% of reached phones were warned in time with one drone and with sixteen, and 95.0% with alerts by distance alone; what the forecast changed was how many phones were told to leave without need.
* When comparing with today's methods, give them the benefit of the doubt, and say where your lead comes from. In the valley it comes mostly from the smouldering phase.
* In our first-crew model, seeing a fire before it spreads is what counts: a camera that sees every fire cut today's escapes by only 2%. Keeping smoulders off public alerts has a price: over 60 valleys, of 1,836 phones the fire reached, 29 to 53 with signal were warned late or never; today's rule, which alerts on smoulders, warned every phone with signal in time. So our pre-signed rules alert on smoulders too.

## What's next

* Mountain search and rescue: the missing-hiker search has been measured only on a meadow and forest edge. Next is the same search over mountain ground, with 1, 4 and 16 drones on fresh seeds.
* Re-tasking in the valley: squads now patrol for 12 minutes, an operator can move drones between them, and a check team picks its next strike partly from what the station has found, but no drone goes back to confirm a doubtful fix or watch a fire. Have the supervisor do that, and measure whether a second look lets the station stand down the alerts for smoulders that never take off.
* Fire weather in the model: couple the fire to its own wind, which no scenario simulates yet (gust fronts stand in for it), and add crown fire and spotting to the forecast.
* Calibrate the airframe, battery and thermal-camera models against flight logs and a real camera's detection range, and the smoke and heat detection models against real footage and sensor logs.
* Write the scout's onboard program and a MAVLink bridge between the simulator's controller and ArduPilot, which the repository does not have yet. Then run the supervisor against real autopilots through a software-in-the-loop bridge, and on a Jetson at the edge.
* Model how people respond to an alert. Today an alert counts as delivered, not as acted on.
* Separate the roles the demo folds into one operator's screen: the drone crew who fly and hold the swarm, fire dispatch who receive the cards, and the alerting authority who signs the trigger rules and holds the cancel, the all-clear and the one-tap approval. Change the aircraft hold from holding in place to clearing to an agreed block or landing.
* Add thermal clutter to the valley (sun-heated rock, vehicles, campfires) and report the false fires and false alerts it causes; today a thermal find is always a true fire.
* Flying it legally. This is a pointer, not legal advice. Halo has an operator hold for crewed firefighting aircraft (shown on one development seed) and logs every order. Real flights would need the fire agency's authorisation, the incident's approval inside any wildfire flight restriction, and approval for one pilot to fly many drones beyond line of sight (in the US, a waiver for a civil operator: the [proposed Part 108 rule](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2025-08-07/html/2025-14992.htm) was not final on 21 October 2026). The README's "Flying it legally" section has the sources.

## Feedback on Nebius Token Factory and NVIDIA models

Answers to the five questions the organisers ask, with the numbers behind them in `evidence/` and `results/`.

* **What we used them for.** Nemotron 3 Nano (`nvidia/NVIDIA-Nemotron-3-Nano-30B-A3B`) and Nemotron 3 Super (`nvidia/nemotron-3-super-120b-a12b`) on Nebius Token Factory, in five roles: the swarm's slow-loop supervisor (a directive every 5 s of simulated time), the mission and valley compilers (plain words to a checked plan or patrol, with refusals), the test engineer in the stress searches, and, for an optional check team, the strike picker (which reported lightning strike a check team looks at next, in a separate measurement). About 13,100 requests over the project up to 14 October, 22.3 million prompt tokens, 3.6 million completion tokens, an estimated $8.23, and about USD 0.33 since, USD 0.047 of it for the valley compiler, USD 0.032 for the valley test engineer and about USD 0.25 for the strike picker.
* **What worked well.** The OpenAI-compatible endpoint worked with a standard-library Python client and JSON mode. Nano with reasoning off answered supervisor calls in 1.17 s on average and Super in 1.37 s, with no failed calls in 6,874 on the held-out runs. The price let us measure instead of guess: a supervised flight cost about $0.004, so we could fly 20 held-out seeds per scenario and configuration and still afford tuning rounds and a 1,000-test stress search.
* **What needs work.** Reasoning is on by default, the reasoning comes back in `message.reasoning` and counts against `max_tokens`; when it runs out, `content` is null with `finish_reason: "length"`, which looks like a broken reply. Our first live check failed that way four times in 43 s before we found `enable_thinking`. A note in the quick-start, or a separate reasoning budget, would save newcomers the paid empty replies. Latency has a long tail: in one tuning round 12 of 826 Nano calls timed out at 8 s, and before we added a whole-call deadline 39 of 540 Super calls took over 30 s, the slowest 64 s. With reasoning on, the strike picker's calls took a median 10.8 s, but two took 143 and 156 s because four attempts in a row timed out at 30 s with no reply before the fifth answered, and the 156 s answer came too late for the pick it was making. A per-request latency hint or a documented p99 would help anyone with a deadline.
* **How onboarding felt, zero to hello world.** Short, with one trap. Key in the environment, the model list returned 25 models (4 Nemotron), and the first supervisor call failed on the reasoning-token default above, four attempts in 43 s (12 October, 22:34 UTC, `evidence/first-attempt/`). With reasoning switched off by a two-line change (`evidence/thinking-off.patch`) the same check passed two minutes later, the supervisor answering in 0.97 s, and a full supervised flight made 30 calls with no fallbacks.
* **Would we build with them again, and why.** Yes. Nemotron 3 Nano is the one we would build on again, as supervisor and compiler; Nemotron 3 Super we would use again as a mission compiler (20 of 20 requests fully right, median 1.3 s, reasoning off) but not as the supervisor, where it cost about five times as much per flight and did worse than the rules in two of nine scenarios. For a decision that can wait a few seconds, a 30B open model at this price and latency is the right tool, and running it on Token Factory meant no GPU to manage and every call logged with a request id for replay. We would keep the same rule: measure it against a conventional baseline on held-out seeds, because on five of nine scenarios the model made no difference, and on the compilers it clearly did (97.6% of fields right with reasoning off against 83.9% for the regex baseline on missions; 99.2% with reasoning on and 95.0% with it off, against 85.3% for hand-written rules on valley jobs). Picking lightning strikes for a check team, it made no measurable difference against a hand-written rule.
